Sample Recommendations

Reference profile: the 204 trips (75 vehicles) in the most fuel-efficient 25% relative to their own vehicle. Each example is the median trip of its pattern, with suggestions ranked by estimated fuel impact. Estimates are per factor and not additive.

No clear inefficiencies detected against the reference.

Stop-and-Go Events (per km)est. +8.8% fuel vs reference
This trip had 1.36 stop-and-go events per km vs 0.52 in the most fuel-efficient trips. In this dataset, trips with more stop-and-go than usual used more fuel in the same vehicle. Where traffic allows, anticipating signals and keeping a steady roll can avoid some full stops.
Idle Fractionest. +3.7% fuel vs reference
The engine idled for 24% of the trip vs 14% in the most fuel-efficient trips. Idling uses fuel without covering distance. Route or departure-time choices that avoid long waits help, and where safe and permitted, switching off during long stationary waits is worth considering.
Hard Braking (per 100 km)est. +2.4% fuel vs reference
This trip had 62 hard braking events per 100 km vs 19 in the most fuel-efficient trips. Hard braking discards speed that fuel was spent building. Looking further ahead allows gentler, earlier deceleration.
Acceleration Variability (g std)est. +1.9% fuel vs reference
Acceleration variability (std 0.075 g vs 0.061 g) is higher than in the most fuel-efficient trips. Smoother, more even speed changes are associated with lower fuel use.
Data: Vehicle Energy Dataset (VED), University of Michigan; ICE vehicles only, a subset of the weekly files. Fuel is estimated from the MAF sensor assuming stoichiometric petrol combustion, so absolute L/100km values are approximate; the analysis relies on relative comparisons within each vehicle.  ·  Limitations: Cluster silhouette 0.27: the patterns overlap, so trips form more of a continuum than distinct groups. Stop-and-go and idling are partly imposed by traffic and signals. Road gradient, weather and vehicle load are not modelled, vehicle class metadata is largely missing, and the data come from one US region. Findings are associations from observational data.
